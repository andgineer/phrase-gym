# Evidence review: answer grading and spaced phrase practice

Reviewed **2026-10-11** in response to the user's request for academic research, an answer card, an effective scheduler and other changes that would improve learning. This is a focused evidence review, not a systematic review. The resulting [design proposal](design/answer-card-and-review-policy.md) is not yet an accepted user decision or implemented functionality.

**User correction:** the learner wants the best scientifically grounded practical method, not a request to validate learning effectiveness. Recommendations must result in usable defaults and ordinary automatic adaptation. No in-app experiment, special assessment session or validation task is wanted. References to scientific uncertainty explain the strength of our recommendation; they must not shift research work onto the learner.

## What effectiveness means here

Optimize accurate, delayed retrieval of useful language per unit of practice time and include spoken practice in the intended situations. Immediate success, familiarity with an answer, predicted recall probability and fluent spoken use are different outcomes. The scientific limits below describe the evidence; they do not create an additional assessment task for the learner.

## Evidence and limits

| Source and access | Relevant evidence | Implication and limit for Phrase Gym |
|---|---|---|
| **Kim & Webb (2022)**, [The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479). Publisher abstract inspected. | 48 experiments, 3,411 participants and 98 effect sizes. Spacing benefited L2 learning; longer spacing favored delayed outcomes over shorter spacing. Equal and expanding schedules were statistically comparable overall. | Use spaced returns. Do not claim that a fixed expanding ladder is uniquely best; task, feedback and test delay matter. |
| **Karpicke & Roediger (2008)**, [The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408). Publisher abstract and author-hosted paper located. | In foreign-vocabulary learning, continued retrieval after an initial correct response improved delayed recall; continued study alone did not produce the same benefit. Learners' predicted performance did not track actual performance. | Continue testing over time, and rate the attempted response rather than familiarity after reveal. This was word-pair learning, not conversational fluency. |
| **Li & Boers (2025)**, [Formats and implementations of exercises for collocation learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.70007). Publisher full text inspected. | 56 ESL learners practiced 32 verb–noun collocations. Studied material followed by cued gap filling produced the best delayed outcomes among the tested combinations; trial-and-error multiple choice was weakest. Cues constrained plausible competing answers. | Preserve productive retrieval, meaningful prior learning and a clear intended target. The written, first-letter-cued task does not validate our spoken whole-phrase workflow or require letter hints. |
| **Sonbul, Macis & Gyllstad (2025; online 2024)**, [Equal versus expanding spacing for L2 collocations](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.3364). Publisher abstract inspected. | 83 learners, 23 collocations: equal 7–7–7 and expanding 1–5–15 schedules both improved learning versus control. Recall did not differ significantly between schedules; recognition favored equal spacing. | Phrase-specific evidence also argues against treating expanding intervals as a universal winner. Those exact intervals are experimental conditions, not app defaults. |
| **Nakata (2017; online 2016)**, [Does repeated practice make perfect?](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280). Publisher abstract inspected. | 98 learners studied 16 word pairs. More within-session retrievals improved raw delayed scores, but one retrieval yielded the largest gain when time on task was controlled. | Separate maximum retention of a small set from efficient learning across a library. Avoid mandatory endless same-session repetitions or a universal repetition quota. |
| **Rawson & Dunlosky (2011)**, [Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707204/), DOI 10.1037/a0023956. Authors' abstract via PubMed inspected. | Three experiments with 533 students learning conceptual material assessed durability after 1–4 months. Relearning across sessions strongly benefited retention; the effect of higher initial mastery criteria diminished with further relearning. | Aim for successful retrieval again on later days. Their three-initial-recalls/three-relearning recommendation is task-specific and is not a universal quota for spoken phrases. |
| **Yanagisawa (2016)**, [Receptive and productive word retrieval practice](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/katejournal/30/0/30_11/_article/-char/en). Publisher abstract inspected. | In a small study of 18 learners and 24 pseudowords, recalling word forms benefited productive tests more than meaning retrieval or visible-word repetition. | Match practice to the desired production task. Small sample and artificial material limit generalization; recognition success alone does not establish production. |
| **Dunlosky & Rawson (2012)**, [Overconfidence produces underachievement](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ964388), DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.08.003. Authors' abstract indexed by ERIC inspected; publisher full text unavailable. | In two definition-learning studies, more accurate judgments of one's response were associated with better delayed retention. | Provide the actual model and clear scoring criteria; grade the original attempt. This does not establish that three rating buttons outperform two or four. |
| **Eglington & Pavlik (2020)**, [Optimizing practice scheduling requires quantitative tracking of individual item performance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567101/). Full text inspected. | Japanese–English word-pair experiments modeled success and time costs. In the scheduling experiment, modeled thresholds .86 and .94 outperformed .40, .98 and the conventional comparator; .70 was not significantly different from the other conditions. | Favor adaptive scheduling and measure time as well as accuracy. These thresholds are not a proven universal 90% optimum or a trial of FSRS, long spoken phrases or month-scale retention. |
| **Settles & Meeder (2016)**, [A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174/). Paper inspected. | Learned memory models improved recall prediction over several baselines. The large online trial's return/engagement metrics concern returning to the app, not simply durable memory. | Prediction can improve scheduling; do not confuse prediction accuracy or app engagement with causal gains in spoken language learning. This is HLR, not FSRS. |
| **Kakitani & Kormos (2024)**, [Distributed practice and L2 fluency development](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263124000251). Publisher full text inspected. | 116 learners completed four fluency-training sessions; 1-day and 7-day spacing produced similar fluency gains at 7- and 28-day posttests. Gains differed across fluency measures. | Include actual spoken practice again on later days as part of normal sessions. Vocabulary recall alone cannot certify automatic speech; do not equate fluency with raw speed or impose a universal response timer. |

The earlier [phrase-focused evidence record](research-memorizing-phrases.md) also covers deliberate collocation spacing and vocalization. Its excluded retracted paper remains excluded. Search results, commercial descriptions and community anecdotes are not used as academic evidence.

## Scheduler implementation evidence is a different category

The current official [Py-FSRS documentation](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/py-fsrs) supports a maintained adaptive memory scheduler, a desired-retention setting, custom learning/relearning steps and parameter fitting. Its rating contract distinguishes forgetting from difficult but successful recall. The [maintainers' tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md) specifically cautions against using Hard for forgotten answers.

These sources document software behavior. They do **not** demonstrate that FSRS is the optimal intervention for our phrases, rotating cues or speech tasks. Recommending it is an engineering judgment informed by research on spacing and adaptive scheduling. The selected 90% desired-retention default is a model prediction at review time, not a promised success rate, mastery score or conversational-fluency percentage. The learner does not need to tune or validate it.

## Changes justified for discussion

1. Replace vague Got it/Try again grading with explicit recall outcomes. Keep the decision to practise again separate from whether the first answer was correct.
2. Use adaptive target-level intervals as the preferred candidate. The previous 1/3/7/14/30-day ladder is superseded historical material, not a comparison policy for the learner to test.
3. Preserve feedback, repair and delayed successful retrieval. Choose usable minutes/retry-cap defaults in the design; do not make them learner-run pilot parameters.
4. Track cue support, actual hidden targets, mode and exposure. A partly supplied sentence must not be presented as proof of wholly independent recall.
5. Use different prepared contexts and actual spoken production during ordinary practice. These remain targeted drills, with sufficient disambiguation and allowed alternatives, not open-ended AI conversation or separate validation sessions.
6. Choose the approach using research on delayed retention and productive use, not marketing claims based only on clicks or same-day success. The learner should not have to run a study to use the app.

No reviewed source selects our exact three buttons, model-help eligibility rules, retry spacing, cue-coverage policy, retention setting or FSRS adaptation. Those are reasoned design choices in the companion proposal. Make those choices for the user; do not make their learning conditional on validating them.
