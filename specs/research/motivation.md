# Motivation and game elements

Question: which game elements help an adult keep practising without distorting learning?

Enjoyment, continued participation and learning are different outcomes. Evidence from classrooms and general tasks can inform adult personal practice, but does not validate a particular Phrase Gym reward system.

- **Sailer & Homner (2020)**, [The gamification of learning: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w). Checked: abstract. Small positive effects on cognitive (g = .49), motivational (g = .36) and behavioral (g = .25) outcomes. Only the cognitive effect held up in the most rigorous studies. **For us:** game elements can help a little; they don't replace good practice.
- **Sailer, Hense, Mayr & Mandl (2017)**, [How gamification motivates](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109059/109059.pdf). Checked: abstract. In a randomized study, badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised the feeling of competence and the sense that the task was meaningful; stories, avatars and teammates raised relatedness. No element raised the feeling of free choice. **For us:** each element works through a specific need. For one learner, a visible record of growing competence is the relevant one.
- **Hanus & Fox (2015)**, [Assessing the effects of gamification in the classroom](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019). Checked: secondary. Over a 16-week course, students with a leaderboard and required badges ended with lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and final exam scores than the same course without them. **For us:** avoid comparison and compulsory collectibles.
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)**, [Extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation: a meta-analysis](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf). Checked: abstract. Expected tangible rewards for doing, completing or performing a task reduced intrinsic motivation; positive feedback that informs about competence did not. **For us:** favor informative progress feedback. Limit: this does not establish that all optional virtual points undermine motivation.
- **Rodrigues et al. (2022)**, [Gamification suffers from the novelty effect but benefits from the familiarization effect](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6). Checked: abstract. 756 students over 14 weeks: the effect of gamification fell after about four weeks, then partly recovered between weeks 6 and 10. **For us:** initial enthusiasm is not evidence of sustained engagement. Limit: no universal one-month expiry follows.
- **Silverman & Barasch (2023)**, [On or off track: how (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029). Checked: abstract. Across seven studies, an intact streak raised continued engagement, and a broken one led people to drop off. The effect was weaker when a broken streak could be repaired. **For us:** a strict daily streak carries a drop-off risk; a weekly target or a repairable streak is safer.
- **Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010)**, [How are habits formed: modelling habit formation in the real world](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674). Checked: abstract. 96 volunteers repeated a chosen daily behaviour in a consistent context for 12 weeks. Automaticity rose along an asymptotic curve, taking 18 to 254 days to plateau, and missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. **For us:** a short session tied to a stable daily cue and a forgiving weekly target; a missed day costs nothing. Limit: eating, drinking and exercise behaviours, not language practice.
- **Zhang & Hasim (2023)**, [Gamification in EFL/ESL instruction: a systematic review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1030790). Checked: abstract. 40 studies; mostly positive but short-term findings, with no pooled effect for language outcomes. Long-term effects are unknown.
- **Lumsden et al. (2017)**, [Attrition from web-based cognitive testing: a repeated measures comparison of gamification techniques](https://www.jmir.org/2017/11/e395/). Checked: primary full-text results/conclusion. Points improved enjoyment, but gamification did not reduce attrition during six days of optional participation; a themed version was less enjoyable. **For us:** points may be an optional preference, but enjoyable presentation alone does not solve dropout. Limits: cognitive tasks, not language learning; short duration with incentives and reminders.

No reviewed study tests the proposed combination on spoken phrase drilling for one adult learner. Short chosen missions, varied situations, visible progress, forgiving weekly targets and optional achievements are design inferences. They belong in a planned post-MVP increment under the [engagement decision](../decision-transfer-and-engagement.md#adult-engagement), with memory grades independent of rewards. A chosen weekly target is not an experimentally established optimum.
