# Phrase prompts, hints and errors

Question: what should the learner see or hear before saying a fixed phrase, how much help should be given, and what happens after a wrong attempt?

No study compares translation prompts, situation prompts and partner-turn prompts for retrieving fixed conversational phrases. The answer below combines the findings that do exist.

## Match practice to use

- **Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977)**, [Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9). Checked: secondary. Memory is best when the processing during learning matches what retrieval demands. **For us:** practise the phrase spoken aloud, in answer to a situation or the other person's line. Limit: a general memory principle, tested with word lists.
- **Li & Taguchi (2014)**, [The effects of practice modality on pragmatic development in L2 Chinese](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-practice-modality-on-pragmatic-development-in-l2-c/). Checked: abstract. 50 intermediate learners practised request forms for four days with input-based or output-based computer practice. Accuracy gains transferred across task types, but speed gains (planning time, speech rate) did not. **For us:** practice should include actual spoken production. Limit: the study compared input with output practice, not translation cues with situation cues, so it does not show that translation-cued production fails.

## Card language

- **Kroll, van Hell, Tokowicz & Green (2010)**, [The revised hierarchical model: a critical review and assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910435/). Checked: full text, conceptual-access discussion. The model's authors conclude that even less proficient learners can understand L2 words without going through the L1, and that production from concepts is the harder direction. **For us:** the model is no basis for banning translation prompts; it describes word processing, not training prompts.
- **Laufer & Girsai (2008)**, [Form-focused instruction in second language vocabulary learning](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amn018). Checked: abstract. High-school learners taught with contrastive analysis and translation tasks in both directions outperformed meaning-focused and non-contrastive form-focused groups on active and passive recall, immediately and a week later. **For us:** a Russian prompt is a legitimate way to state an intention while German forms are learned. Limit: translation was not isolated from the contrastive teaching, and conversational speed was not measured.
- **Hu & Nation (2000)** and **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)**: coverage thresholds for reading whole texts; see [when a gap can work](words-in-context.md#when-a-gap-can-work). **For us:** a prompt must be understood without learning incidental material, so the card language follows what the learner can read, not a fixed percentage.

## One phrase per moment

- **Poirier, Nairne et al. (2012)**, [Memory as discrimination](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/2519/), building on Nairne (2002) and on cue overload (Watkins & Watkins, 1975). Checked: abstract. Retrieval succeeds when a cue singles out the target; a cue that fits many stored items works worse. **For us:** when several drilled phrases fit the same moment, the prompt is overloaded. Drilling one preferred phrase per moment avoids this without padding situations with artificial register or emotion, which would turn them into paraphrase codes for the answer. Limit: laboratory memory tasks; cue discrimination says nothing about whether natural conversation has one right answer.
- **Li & Boers (2025)**, [Formats and implementations of exercises for collocation learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.70007). Checked: full text. 56 ESL learners, 32 verb–noun collocations. Study followed by cued gap-filling gave the best delayed scores among the tested formats, with modest differences. The first letter was supplied "to prevent learners from supplying appropriate responses other than the target verbs." **For us:** recall of a designated item needs a constrained task; here the constraint is "your phrase for this moment". Limit: written collocations, not spoken whole phrases.

## Situation plus partner turn

- **Bardovi-Harlig, Mossman & Su (2017)**, [The effect of corpus-based instruction on pragmatic routines](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/12991145-c5d6-4eb5-b7c4-d2a228eb8d84/content). Checked: full text. The screen shows the topic and the learner's intended move ("your position is different from your classmate's"). The classmate's turn then plays as audio only, and the learner answers aloud within 10 seconds. Four lessons of noticing plus oral game practice raised the oral production of agreement, disagreement and clarification routines; a repeated-test control did not improve. The paper summarizes two earlier studies: [Bardovi-Harlig, Mossman & Vellenga (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168814541739) found the same gains with oral practice, and Bardovi-Harlig & Vellenga (2012) found that noticing alone improved oral production of 30 conventional expressions. **For us:** a short situation plus the other person's line, answered aloud, is how the field elicits conventional expressions; learners were taught the expressions first. Limit: any taught routine of the right function counted, the setting was classroom instruction, and the format is precedent rather than the proven cause of the gains.
- **Bardovi-Harlig (2009)**, [Conventional expressions as a pragmalinguistic resource](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00525.x). Checked: secondary. 172 speakers did an aural recognition task and an oral production task in context. Recognizing an expression was necessary, but not sufficient, for producing it. **For us:** reading or recognizing phrases does not make the learner use them; spoken production practice is required.
- Openers have no partner turn. A written situation is a scaffold for them; no study shows that recalling an opener from a situation transfers to starting a real call.

## Vary the prompt

- **Butowska-Buczyńska, Kliś, Zawadzka & Hanczakowski (2024)**, [The role of variable retrieval in effective learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2413511121). Checked: abstract; materials as described in an external review of the methods. Finnish words were embedded in Polish context sentences, which served as retrieval cues for Polish meaning recall. Changing the sentences across practice beat repeating the same one, with or without feedback, immediately and after 24 hours. Learners judged constant cues to be more effective, which was wrong. **For us:** add genuinely different situations over time and rotate them; three paraphrases of one situation are weak variation. Limit: word meanings, one-day retention; no number of prompts follows from it.
- **Goode, Geraci & Roediger (2008)**, [Superiority of variable to repeated practice in transfer on anagram solution](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5287848_Superiority_of_variable_to_repeated_practice_in_transfer_on_anagram_solution), and **Butler, Black-Maier, Raley & Marsh (2017)**, [Retrieving and applying knowledge to different examples promotes transfer](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c8baca1e5f7d136349ea789/t/5e739a3d46338e7dec37aaf6/1584634429337/Butler+et+al+2017.pdf). Checked: secondary. Practising with varied versions of a problem or question transferred better to new ones than repeating the same version.

## Study first when the phrase is unfamiliar

- **Strong & Boers (2019)**, [Weighing up exercises on phrasal verbs: retrieval versus trial-and-error practices](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12579). Checked: abstract. Studying phrasal verbs before retrieving them beat guessing followed by feedback; delayed scores were low in every condition. Of the trial-and-error group's later errors, 70% repeated their own wrong exercise answers; this is the share of later errors, not the chance that an initial error persists.
- **Strong & Boers (2018)**, [The error in trial and error: exercises on phrasal verbs](https://core.ac.uk/works/9193838) (TESOL Quarterly). Checked: abstract. 140 Japanese EFL students. Study-then-retrieve beat trial-and-error on immediate and one-week tests. Despite feedback, 25% of wrong exercise answers reappeared a week later.
- **Strong & Leeming (2024)**, [Evaluating the application of a gap-fill exercise on the learning of phrasal verbs: do errors help or hinder learning?](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.3248). Checked: abstract. 118 EFL learners did a gap-fill exercise either with the answer key at hand or with the key only afterwards; both gave similar retention on productive and receptive tests. Recollecting the initial guess went with much better recall of the target.
- **Boers, Demecheleer, Coxhead & Webb (2014)**, [Gauging the effects of exercises on verb–noun collocations](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168813505389), and **Boers, Dang & Strong (2017)**, [Comparing the effectiveness of phrase-focused exercises](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168816651464). Checked: abstract. Matching exercises taught wrong pairings despite feedback; working with intact phrases beat choosing or completing the verb.
- **Metcalfe (2017)**, [Learning from errors](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf). Checked: full text. For typical learners, errors followed by corrective feedback can help, and confidently held errors are corrected best. Limit: mostly factual and semantic material.

**For us:** error effects depend on the task, prior knowledge and feedback; there is no categorical boundary between facts and phrase forms. A genuinely unfamiliar phrase is studied before its first recall. A phrase the learner already studied, for example in a completed course, starts with an attempt, and the reference follows at once.

## Least help that still succeeds

- **Finley, Benjamin, Hays, Bjork & Kornell (2011)**, [Benefits of accumulating versus diminishing cues in recall](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Finley_Benjamin_Hays_RBjork_Kornell_2011.pdf). Checked: full text. English–Iñupiaq word pairs. Without feedback, cues that shrank across trials gave the best final recall (.40 vs .25 for growing cues and .29 for study only). With feedback, both cue schedules beat study only. Limit: word pairs, retention over minutes.
- **Fiechter & Benjamin (2018)**, [Diminishing-cues retrieval practice](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/diminishing-cues-retrieval-practice-a-memory-enhancing-technique-/). Checked: abstract. For hard items without feedback, fading cues beat plain testing and restudy. With feedback, fading cues and plain testing were equal.
- **Fiechter & Benjamin (2019)**, [Techniques for scaffolding retrieval practice: the costs and benefits of adaptive versus diminishing cues](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-019-01617-6). Checked: abstract. Six experiments with English–Iñupiaq pairs. Cues accumulated within a trial as the learner needs them helped most where plain testing failed. Where plain testing worked, adaptive cues, diminishing cues and plain testing gave about the same benefit, and diminishing cues took the least time.
- **Carpenter & DeLosh (2006)**, [Impoverished cue support enhances subsequent retention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16752591/). Checked: abstract. Fewer cue letters on practice tests gave better final recall.

**For us:** with feedback after every attempt, a multi-step hint ladder has no demonstrated benefit. Hint stays on request, as one partial cue chosen for the phrase and then the full answer. No study identifies which part of a multiword phrase is the best cue; the choice is content design. In the vault's 63 English phrases, 40 share their first word with another phrase, so a first word is often a weak cue, but it is not forbidden.

## Say it aloud

- **Hashizaki**, [vocalization and formulaic sequence learning](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688231224040). Checked: abstract. 32 Japanese learners. Reading collocations aloud improved recognition of form and meaning relative to silent reading, including after a week. Limit: recognition, not spoken recall.
- **Forrin & MacLeod (2018)**, production effect, [university summary](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/news/study-finds-reading-information-aloud-yourself-improves). Checked: secondary. Reading aloud to oneself beat hearing others read and silent reading.

**For us:** saying the phrase aloud belongs in the first meeting and in repair after a wrong or effortful attempt. Nothing shows that an extra say-along after every successful recall adds to memory, and it never counts as recall.

## Model-prepared prompts

- **Attali et al. (2022)**, [The interactive reading task: transformer-based automatic item generation](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2022.903077). Checked: abstract. Language models generated hundreds of passages and thousands of questions for the Duolingo English Test practice test. Human review and test-taker statistics showed the approach was feasible.
- No study measures model-written prompts or lead-in lines for phrase drills, or their error rate. **For us:** use bounded existing-target checks, retain useful alternatives for lookup, and suspend/recreate bad cards during practice. Representative semantic acceptance precedes unattended use, but there is no routine learner approval of every prompt beside its answer: that would itself be study.
