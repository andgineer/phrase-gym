# Words: translation, context and confusable pairs

Question: how should words without a single translation be learned, by a beginner with translation (German A1) and by an advanced learner without it (English B2 learning rarer words)?

## Flashcards versus context

- **Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020)**, [How effective are intentional vocabulary-learning activities? A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671). Checked: full text, Tables 2–3 and results. 22 studies, 100 effect sizes. Estimated proportions learned:

  | Activity | Immediate meaning | Immediate form | Delayed meaning | Delayed form |
  |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
  | Flashcards | 77.0% | 66.1% | 73.4% | 32.0% |
  | Word lists | 73.2% | 70.1% | 47.9% | 21.8% |
  | Writing | 54.8% | 36.8% | 31.9% | 18.0% |
  | Fill-in-the-blanks | 43.1% | 18.4% | 23.9% | 18.3% |

  Flashcards retained significantly more delayed meaning than the other activities, but that estimate comes from two studies. Delayed form-recall differences were not significant for any pair. The rows aggregate heterogeneous studies (I² ≈ 97%), not randomized arms of one cohort, so the columns are not a forgetting curve. **For us:** retrieving the word–meaning link, flashcard style, stays the core; a gapped sentence is one prompt format, not proof of independent use. Limit: test formats favored form–meaning learning, treatments lasted at most a day, and collocation or grammatical use was not settled.
- **Elgort (2011)**, [Deliberate learning and vocabulary acquisition in a second language](https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf). Checked: abstract. Words learned deliberately from flashcards produced form, repetition and semantic priming, and were processed more automatically than low-frequency known words. **For us:** flashcard learning builds real, fast word knowledge; it is not only a "test skill". Limit: whether that knowledge appears in natural use was not tested.
- **Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019)**, [The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343). Checked: secondary. Across 26 studies, the number of encounters correlated moderately with incidental learning (r = .34). **For us:** reading and listening supply encounters, but slowly per word. They complement deliberate practice rather than replace it.
- **Nation & Yamamoto (2012)**, [Applying the four strands to language learning](https://vuw-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/yamamoto-four-strands.pdf). Checked: secondary. A balanced course gives roughly equal time to meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. **For us:** Phrase Gym covers language-focused learning and fluency. Reading, listening and real conversation remain necessary outside it.
- **Keyword mnemonic.** [Wang & Thomas (1995)](https://archive.memory-key.com/research/Wang95), checked: secondary. The method raised immediate recall, but in between-subject designs long-term forgetting was greater than with rote rehearsal. **For us:** not a default technique.

## When a gap can work

- **Hu & Nation (2000)** and **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)**, [lexical thresholds for comprehension](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/206). Checked: secondary. About 95% known words gives minimal comprehension of a text, and 98% gives adequate comprehension. Replications find comprehension rising roughly steadily with coverage rather than at a hard cutoff. **For us:** in a ten-word sentence one unknown word moves coverage by ten points, so no threshold transfers to a single prompt. The requirement is functional: the learner understands the intended message and grammar without learning incidental material. For a beginner the context itself is unknown, so German cards use the Russian meaning of a whole chunk.
- **de Groot & Keijzer (2000)**, [What is hard to learn is easy to forget](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00110). Checked: secondary. Cognates and concrete words were learned better and forgotten less than non-cognates and abstract words. **For us:** shared Slavic words help a Russian speaker read Serbian, but cognates alone do not establish comprehension: unknown grammar and false friends such as *vrata* (door, not gate) and *život* (life, not belly) can defeat familiar-looking wording.

## Translation or L2 explanation

- **Prince (1996)**, [Second language vocabulary learning: the role of context versus translations as a function of proficiency](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1996.tb05468.x), discussed with a replication in [this review](https://english.fullerton.edu/publications/clnArchives/pdf/jahangard-VocabLrn.pdf). Checked: secondary. Learning with translations gave better recall than learning from context, especially for weaker learners, while context-learned words transferred better into new sentences. The replication's low-proficiency learners could not transfer to new contexts. [Alroe & Reinders](https://api.equinoxpub.com/articles/34212) criticize the method. **For us:** keep the native translation available for a beginner, and add context so the word can be used beyond the card.
- **Joyce (2016)**, [L2 vocabulary learning and testing: student proficiency and the use of L1 translations versus L2 definitions](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NELTA/article/view/20205). Checked: abstract. 48 Japanese learners studied 200 academic words over ten weeks, half with L1 translations and half with L2 definitions. Proficiency did not interact with the study language; it interacted with the testing language, higher-ability learners doing relatively better on L2 definition tests. **For us:** no evidence that stronger learners learn better from L2 definitions. English-only help for English rests on the learner's preference and comprehensibility.
- **Laufer & Hadar (1997)**, [Monolingual, bilingual and "bilingualised" dictionaries](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/assessing-the-effectiveness-of-monolingual-bilingual-and-bilingua/). Checked: abstract. 123 learners met 15 rare words through a learner's dictionary entry, a translation, or both, and were tested on comprehension and on using the words in sentences. The suitable format depended on the learner's dictionary skill.
- **Laufer & Girsai (2008)** and **Kroll et al. (2010)**: translation with contrastive teaching was effective, and the revised hierarchical model does not imply that translation practice traps learners; see [card language](phrase-prompts.md#card-language). No study found shows that an occasional L1 gloss harms an advanced learner.

## Several contexts, practised in the needed direction

- **Bolger, Balass, Landen & Perfetti (2008)**, [Context variation and definitions in learning the meanings of words](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/Context%20variation%20Bolger%20et%20al.pdf). Checked: abstract. Several different contexts taught a word's core meaning better than one repeated context, and a definition conveyed more than context alone. **For us:** two or three different short contexts per word, plus a short definition.
- **Butowska-Buczyńska et al. (2024)**: changing the context sentence used as the retrieval cue beat a constant sentence; see [phrase prompts](phrase-prompts.md#vary-the-prompt).
- **Webb (thesis)**, [Investigating the effects of learning tasks on vocabulary knowledge](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Investigating_the_Effects_of_Learning_Tasks_on_Vocabulary_Knowledge/16945963). Checked: abstract. Receptive learning built mainly receptive knowledge and productive learning built productive knowledge. A single glossed context sentence added little over the word pair, and word pairs gave surprisingly large gains across aspects of word knowledge. **For us:** understanding a chunk and saying it from its meaning are different demands, so successful understanding must not lengthen the schedule that saying depends on. The schedule is built around saying from the first meeting.
- **Griffin & Harley (1996)**, [List learning of second language vocabulary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/list-learning-of-second-language-vocabulary/F00040624B9C1370140CF398F9A1E0B0). Checked: abstract. The L1→L2 direction was the more versatile when both production and comprehension were needed; later literature has no consensus. **For us:** start saying as soon as the word is understood and has been said once with support.
- Yanagisawa (2016) found recalling word forms helped production more than recalling meanings; see [spacing and grading](spacing-and-grading.md#retrieval-and-repetition).

## Confusable words

- **Laufer (1988)**, [The concept of synforms (similar lexical forms) in vocabulary acquisition](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/the-concept-of-synforms-similar-lexical-forms-in-vocabulary-acqui/). Checked: abstract. Words that differ by an affix or by one sound are a systematic source of learner errors. **For us:** pairs such as *farther/further* and prefix choices such as *non-/un-* need an explicit contrast note.
- **Nakata & Suzuki (2019)**, [Effects of massing and spacing on the learning of semantically related and unrelated words](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219). Checked: abstract. 133 Japanese university students, 48 word pairs. Related and unrelated words gave similar posttest scores, but related words caused more interference errors, and spacing helped unrelated words more than related ones. The paper builds on Tinkham (1993), Waring (1997) and Nation (2000), who report that new near-synonyms or words of one semantic set learned together interfere with each other. **For us:** a mild separation is justified: the second of a confusable pair waits until the first has been recalled once on a later day. Reopening the app is not a meaningful learning boundary, so the rule is not tied to sessions. Lessons are grouped by situation, not by semantic set.
- **Brunmair & Richter (2019)**, [Similarity matters: a meta-analysis of interleaved learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209). Checked: abstract. Across 59 studies, interleaving beat blocking overall (g = .42), most for paintings (g = .67) and other visual material, less for mathematics (g = .34). In the studies based on words, blocking did better (g = −0.39), and the authors advise caution with interleaving for words and expository texts. **For us:** no support for dedicated mixed drills of confusable words; once both are known they simply meet in ordinary reviews.
- **Cambridge Dictionary**, [further or farther](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/word-choice-further-or-farther). Checked: secondary, via the external review. Both express physical distance; only *further* has the sense "more, additional" (*further notice*, *further information*). **For us:** contrast notes explain overlap as well as difference, and prompts never treat *farther* as the only answer for distance.
