# Automatic use of phrases

Question: how can useful expressions become available for independently composed speech?

There is actionable evidence of increased expression use in unscripted classroom speech. That is stronger than card recall, but does not establish durable transfer to everyday conversation or validate Phrase Gym. Expression use, speaking fluency and long-term natural use are separate outcomes.

## Instruction leading to independently composed speech

- **Thomson, Coxhead, Boers & Warren (2023)**, [Increasing use of multi-word expressions in conversation through a fluency workshop](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2023.102994). Checked: primary-source abstract and retrieved methods/results sections. Over six weeks, 65 Japanese university EFL learners practised 30 expressions through a combined workshop including noticing, shadowing, reconstruction and role-play; 51 controls followed ordinary linked-skills classes. Expression use increased more in the treatment group during unscripted paired role-play, without displaying or requiring the target wording. **For us:** support a progression from learning expressions to choosing them in self-generated speech. Limits: quasi-experimental; the speech analysis retained only 46 learners; situations resembled the taught travel contexts; partial expressions counted, and complete four-word expressions were rare. General fluency did not improve more than in controls. This does not isolate one effective exercise or establish lasting everyday transfer for a B2 adult.
- **McGuire & Larson-Hall (2018; journal issue dated 2017)**, [Teaching formulaic sequences in the classroom: effects on spoken fluency](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1271/1106/1615). Checked: primary full-text methods, results and limitations. Over five weeks, 11 learners received phrase-focused noticing and speaking practice; eight controls had task-based speaking without that focus. The treatment group gained more in expression use, speech rate and mean length of run in conversations. **For us:** deliberate phrase practice can contribute beyond recognition. Limits: small groups, different teachers, study-abroad setting, the same test topic before/after and subjective identification of formulaic sequences. Native-speaker fluency ratings did not show a clear significant benefit.
- **Thomson (2024)**, [Improving classroom teaching and learning of multi-word expressions for conversational use through action research with learner feedback](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages9110336). Checked: primary-source methods/results and activity descriptions. Across three workshop cohorts, the initial version improved phrase knowledge without a clear conversational-use advantage; a later version also improved use. Changes included scaffolding, clearer activity instructions and time to reflect on recordings. **For us:** support learners during early production and progressively move toward freer use. Limits: bundled changes across cohorts do not identify which change caused improvement; the later results include the 2023 study, not an independent replication.
- **Duong et al. (2021)**, [Incidental lexical mining in task repetition](https://backoffice.biblio.ugent.be/download/8720893/8720894). Checked: primary author-manuscript abstract. Ninety learners performed oral tasks with input, repeated input or no input. Learners reused words and expressions from input, but fewer expressions appeared on the repeated task two days later; extra input exposure did not improve later lexical mining. **For us:** immediate reuse after a video/model is supported practice, not durable acquisition. Limit: repeated oral tasks, not a long-term conversational intervention.
- **Van Lancker Sidtis et al. (2015)**, [The formulaic schema in the minds of two generations of native speakers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573498/). Checked: primary-source abstract and discussion excerpt. Cloze tasks examined fixed expressions, open slots in schemata and novel sentences across two generations of native speakers. Results support interplay between familiar expressions and novel wording. **For us:** identify reusable chunks within complete examples and vary the surrounding cue context; avoid claiming that fluent speakers only reproduce memorized sentences. This motivates the learning target, not a proven gap-cue method. Limit: not a teaching intervention.

## Supporting mechanisms and narrower findings

- **Li (2013)**, [Amount of practice and pragmatic development of request-making in L2 Chinese](https://benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.36.04li). Checked: secondary. Four practice instances were enough to improve accuracy, but more than eight were needed to improve speed. **For us:** a phrase recalled correctly is not yet automatic; speed needs further spaced spoken practice. Limit: one study, requests, short span.
- **Li & Taguchi (2014)**: speed gains did not transfer between input-based and output-based practice; see [phrase prompts](phrase-prompts.md#match-practice-to-use).
- **de Jong & Perfetti (2011)**, [Fluency training in the ESL classroom](https://sites.pitt.edu/~perfetti/PDF/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf). Checked: full text. 24 ESL learners did the 4/3/2 task: the same talk told in 4, 3 and 2 minutes. Learners who repeated the same topic kept their fluency gains on new topics, and they reused more word strings. **For us:** repeated spoken production is worth including. Limit: small; repeated monologues do not establish that memorized dialogue runs or fixed-phrase cards transfer to spontaneous use.
- **Thai & Boers (2016)**, [Repeating a monologue under increasing time pressure: effects on fluency, complexity, and accuracy](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.232). Checked: abstract. 20 Vietnamese EFL students gave the same talk three times. Shrinking time raised fluency most but brought no significant gain in complexity or accuracy, as learners duplicated their earlier wording; constant time gave smaller fluency gains with modest gains in complexity and accuracy. **For us:** do not require time pressure; use untimed spoken practice. The evidence does not establish that untimed cards produce automatic conversational speed. Limit: the study does not show that rushed errors become fixed.
- **Kakitani & Kormos (2024)**, [Distributed practice and L2 fluency development](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263124000251). Checked: full text. 116 learners did four fluency sessions; 1-day and 7-day spacing gave similar gains at 7 and 28 days. **For us:** spoken practice belongs in ordinary spaced sessions; no exact interval is privileged.
- **Wood (2009)**, [Effects of focused instruction of formulaic sequences on fluent expression](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/view/19898). Checked: abstract. Single learner: instruction and practice of formulaic sequences increased fluency. Limit: one case.
- **ACCESS** (Gatbonton & Segalowitz), applied in [Dao, Iwashita & Gatbonton (2017)](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/623465). Checked: abstract. Communicative tasks are built so that learners repeat the same formulaic utterances many times in genuine exchanges, aiming at automaticity. **For us:** meaningful opportunities to speak matter, but this does not require artificial dialogue runs in the app. Limit: classroom task design; transfer to a personal card trainer is untested.
- **Boers & Lindstromberg (2012)**, [Experimental and intervention studies on formulaic sequences in a second language](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259428229_Experimental_and_Intervention_Studies_on_Formulaic_Sequences_in_a_Second_Language). Checked: secondary. Using formulaic sequences goes with more fluent speech. Awareness-raising that relies on later incidental pick-up was not shown to work, and retention and productive use were underwhelming without deliberate productive practice.
- **Webb, Newton & Chang (2013)**, [Incidental learning of collocation](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2012.00729.x). Checked: abstract. Reading while listening with 1, 5, 10 or 15 encounters: more encounters gave more learning, and 15 gave sizeable gains. **For us:** phrases need many encounters; deliberate spaced practice supplies them. Limit: incidental learning.
- **Bardovi-Harlig (2009)**: recognition is necessary but not sufficient for production; see [phrase prompts](phrase-prompts.md#situation-plus-partner-turn).
- **Wang (2023)**, [Text memorization and argumentative writing](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1126194). Checked: abstract. 33 English majors memorized seven model texts, and their argumentative writing improved. **For us:** this does not justify mandatory dialogue memorization for spoken use. Limit: writing, not speech.

The workshop findings above do not isolate shadowing as the cause of conversational uptake or establish an AI partner as equivalent to a human interlocutor. Those mechanisms remain unverified for this app.

## Design implication

Keep spaced spoken retrieval as the first MVP. Repeatable bounded Extra practice extends that recall loop without becoming freer speech. A later activity could use supported personal responses and repeated meaningful tasks with variation before unscripted responses, but its function still needs design. This progression is a synthesis of the evidence, not a validated app protocol. Do not require phrase insertion where another response is more natural, or equate a phrase count with conversational competence. The [practice decision](../decision-transfer-and-engagement.md) and [delivery ladder](../plan/implementation-plan.md) state the currently scheduled behavior.
